Triple coronal hard X-ray source observed by STIX during a failed filament eruption

Tomasz Mrozek, Marek Stęślicki, Sylwester Kołomański, Krzysztof Barczynski
Submitted 15 Sep 2026, astro-ph.SR
A&A accepted
arXivPDFADS

Case study of the occulted M1.3 flare SOL2022-02-15T1815 in which STIX (MARLIN-reconstructed), EUI and AIA reveal a transient triple non-thermal (20–28 keV) coronal source during the braking of a failed filament eruption and three long-lived thermal coronal sources, two attributed to direct heating by eruption–overlying-field reconnection.

Same scientific problem3×0.30

It addresses where and when electrons are accelerated and plasma heated in a flare, using STIX timing of non-thermal hardening against eruption kinematics. Its focus is a failed-eruption coronal-source morphology rather than rapid variability or QPPs.

Same methodology3×0.25

Time-resolved OSPEX thermal + thin-target fits, STIX count-based imaging with algorithm cross-checks, and AIA DEM-to-X-ray forward modelling are directly transferable. No high-cadence timing, cross-correlation or periodicity analysis is done.

Same data / instruments5×0.15

Uses exactly STIX pixel data and EUI 174 Å images, with AIA and GOES support, from a 2022 Solar Orbiter event.

Same physical phenomenon3×0.20

Covers coronal non-thermal emission, spectral hardening and thermal evolution in an M-class flare. The setting is an occulted, coronal-only failed eruption, not footpoint/ribbon-driven variability.

Potential future utility3×0.10

Useful as a reference for STIX imaging of weak coronal sources in occulted events, for low-count reconstruction limits, and as a possible case-study event. It is less central to a QPP or timing-focused thesis.

SummaryThe authors combine STIX pixel data (20 s cadence, 4–28 keV), EUI 174 Å and AIA EUV images of a flare 8–10° behind the…

The authors combine STIX pixel data (20 s cadence, 4–28 keV), EUI 174 Å and AIA EUV images of a flare 8–10° behind the limb as seen from Solar Orbiter, so footpoints are occulted and weak coronal HXR sources can be imaged. Spatially integrated OSPEX fits (thermal + thin-target) show the non-thermal index dropping below 6 at ~18:03:30 UT, coincident with peak velocity and onset of deceleration of the eruption fronts (up to 500 km/s), with hardening persisting until ~18:16:30 UT (Fig. 6). A 20–28 keV triple source appears in only one 140 s interval (18:02:10–18:04:30), co-spatial with expanding parts of the eruption and a break in the eruption front seen in EUI difference images (Fig. 7). In 6–10 keV, three thermal sources are imaged at 40 s cadence; MARLIN images are cross-checked against EM, MEM_GE and VIS_FWDFIT_PSO (Fig. 4) and against AIA-DEM-predicted 11 keV maps (Fig. 8). The higher sources (60–70 and 90–100 Mm) cool over ~8000–10,000 s, and a GOES diagnostic diagram suggests weak prolonged heating in the decay phase (Fig. 10). For the researcher, it is a worked example of linking STIX spectral-index evolution with EUV kinematics and of STIX imaging of weak coronal sources, but it contains no QPP, energy-dependent timing or ribbon analysis.

Abstract{Observations reveal hard X-ray sources in the solar corona with morphologies and locations distinct from typical solar…

{Observations reveal hard X-ray sources in the solar corona with morphologies and locations distinct from typical solar flares. One expected mechanisms for hard X-ray (HXR) production in the solar corona is eruptions of various types. Failed eruptions are promising targets for detecting non-flare-related HXR sources because most of their energy is expected to be dissipated in the corona. } {We investigated the SOL2022-0 to determine the nature of coronal HXR sources associated with the event.} {We used Solar Orbiter's STIX, EUI, and SDO AIA observations. We reconstructed STIX images using the MARLIN algorithm. We used EUI data to cross-check the view of the event from two vantage points. The AIA images enabled kinematic analysis and reconstruction of differential emission measure (DEM) maps. We then used the AIA DEM to predict X-ray emission maps and compared them with STIX images. } {We find temporal and spatial correlations between the failed eruption and HXR sources. During the braking of the filament eruption, we observed non-thermal HXR emission from coronal sources along the path of the eruption. Thermal HXR emission was concentrated in three coronal sources formed with two separate mechanisms: chromospheric evaporation and direct heating. Directly heated sources are the result of the interaction of the eruption with the overlying magnetic field, and were observed to cool for an extremely long time.} {The HXR emission sources related to the interaction between the eruption and the overlying magnetic field might be typical structures in all flares. However, they appear to be weak, since their emission is only 5-20~\% of that from the flare coronal source. If footpoint sources were not occulted, then these weak coronal emission sources would not be visible due to the low dynamic range of HXR telescopes.}

  • A documented STIX event (SOL2022-02-15, M1.3, occulted from SO by 8–10°) with 20 s STIX data, 40 s imaging cadence, and EUI/AIA context (Sects. 2–3).
  • Time series of STIX-derived T, EM and thin-target delta (Fig. 6D–F) aligned with AIA 171 Å eruption-front velocities (Fig. 6B), a template for kinematics–spectral timing comparisons.
  • Comparison of four STIX imaging algorithms (MARLIN, EM, VIS_FWDFIT_PSO, MEM_GE) on the same interval (Fig. 4), using collimators 3–10.
  • A method to forward-model thermal X-ray maps from AIA DEMs (Plowman & Caspi 2020 + Culhane & Acton 1970 continuum) to test the reality of faint STIX sources (Sect. 3.3, Fig. 8).
  • A demonstration that STIX imaging gives qualitatively sensible results from ~1733 counts (~18 counts per stripe) (Sect. 3.3).
  • A practical recipe for comparing EUI and AIA at ~17° separation without reprojection, with ~10 arcsec agreement and ~10 arcsec STIX pointing accuracy after the aspect solution (Sect. 2.4, Fig. 5).
  • A GOES T–EM diagnostic diagram (QSS vs OFF branches) as a decay-phase heating diagnostic (Fig. 10).

Evidence

The thin-target delta drops below 6 at ~18:03:30 UT, reaching a minimum of 5.3, coincident with peak eruption-front speed and onset of deceleration; hardening persists until 18:16:30 UT (Sect. 3.2, Sect. 4, Fig. 6B,F).

Inference

Spectral-index evolution from spatially integrated STIX spectra can be timed against EUV kinematics at ~20–40 s resolution, linking acceleration episodes to dynamic restructuring. This is a direct analogue of the spectral-evolution diagnostics the researcher plans, though here only in coronal (occulted) emission.

Recommendation

Adopt the side-by-side kinematics/spectral-index format for case studies, but use finer time bins and formal cross-correlation where count rates allow.

medium confidence When fitting STIX spectra of limb or occulted flares, test thin- against thick-target and double-thermal models explicitly, and report resi…
Evidence

Thin-target fits gave the lowest χ² and most random residuals versus thick-target, broken power-law and double-thermal models for the occulted event (Sect. 2.3.1, Sect. 3.2, Fig. 3: T = 21 MK, δ = 5.6, Ec = 15 keV).

Inference

For occulted or coronal-dominated events, model choice for the non-thermal component materially affects inferred electron parameters. The thermal/non-thermal separation above ~20 keV here rests on residual randomness rather than a clear χ² margin.

Recommendation

When fitting STIX spectra of limb or occulted flares, test thin- against thick-target and double-thermal models explicitly, and report residual behaviour (e.g. with sunkit-spex in Python).

high confidence Use multiple algorithms and an independent forward model (e.g. AIA DEM-predicted X-ray maps) before interpreting faint STIX sources, and tr…
Evidence

Four reconstruction algorithms give morphologically similar three-source images at 6–10 keV (Fig. 4). Sources below 10% of peak are flagged as unreliable, and source 3 is described as 'not well supported' before 18:20 UT, when it sits at ~20:1 relative to source 1 (Sect. 3.3).

Inference

STIX dynamic range (~10:1–20:1) limits detection of secondary sources. This matters when trying to localise where variability originates if a bright source dominates.

Recommendation

Use multiple algorithms and an independent forward model (e.g. AIA DEM-predicted X-ray maps) before interpreting faint STIX sources, and treat sub-10% features as tentative.

Evidence

The non-thermal triple source appears in a single 140 s interval only. The authors state that the event was too weak for imaging spectroscopy and that projection effects make interpretation 'challenging, if not impossible' (Sect. 4).

Inference

The headline triple non-thermal source is a single-snapshot detection. The physical scenario (erosion of the flux rope by reconnection with the overlying field) is speculative rather than established.

Recommendation

Cite the scenario as a possibility, not a result. If the event is used, re-examine the 20–28 keV images with the now-calibrated finer collimators and current STIX software.

Evidence

Source 1, identified as the flare loop-top source, shows two brightness maxima (~18:07 and 18:15 UT). A rising blob in DEM-derived X-ray maps is interpreted as evaporation linked to energy release 3–5 min earlier (Sect. 4, Fig. 6C, Movie4).

Inference

The double-peaked STIX/GOES light curve (Fig. 1) is attributed to distinct energy-release episodes in a single coronal source. This is a minutes-scale variability example rather than QPP, with only a qualitative mechanism.

Recommendation

If the event enters a statistical sample, note that its lightcurve morphology is governed by occultation and eruption dynamics. Footpoint-based timing diagnostics are unavailable for it.

Caveats6No QPP, periodicity, energy-dependent delay or high-cadence (<20 s) timing analysis is performed. Its connect…
  • No QPP, periodicity, energy-dependent delay or high-cadence (<20 s) timing analysis is performed. Its connection to rapid variability is indirect.
  • The event is occulted from Solar Orbiter, so footpoints and ribbons are absent from STIX; results do not inform ribbon-localised variability.
  • The non-thermal triple source rests on one 140 s image at 20–28 keV, and source 3 is near the dynamic-range limit; the physical interpretation is explicitly scenario-level.
  • Analysis uses IDL/SSW (OSPEX, AIA deconvolution) rather than Python/SunPy tools, so it requires porting.
  • Only collimators 3–10 were used. The DEM-based X-ray prediction ignores lines and non-thermal emission and uses filters sensitive to cool plasma, so it is qualitative.
  • Cooling-time and decay-phase heating inferences come from a simplified AIA appearance-time analysis and GOES Sun-as-a-star diagnostics, without spatial attribution.
