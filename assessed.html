3.2 Exploring an X1.8 flare and associated quasi-periodic pulsations using SDO and Aditya-L1 observations, alongside NFFF extrapolation

Pawan Kumar, Sanjay Kumar, Sadashiv, Sachin Rajkapoor Kanaujiya et al. · submitted 2026-10-06 direct
21 pages, 9 figures accepted for publication in Solar Physics
arXiv:2610.08124PDFADS

Multi-instrument case study of the SOL2024-10-09 X1.8 flare that reports a ~4-min QPP in Aditya-L1/HEL1OS 20–50 keV hard X-rays, places the pulsating HXR source on one ribbon (R1) using a single STIX MEM_GE image plus AIA ribbon lightcurves, and interprets it with an NFFF extrapolation (flux rope, 3D null, QSLs) as recurrent reconnection.

The authors use the GOES-18 1–8 Å curve to time the X1.8 flare in AR 13848 (01:25–01:56–02:43 UT). AIA 94/131 Å images show a pre-flare sigmoid, and AIA 304/1600 Å images show four ribbons or brightenings (R1–R4) (Figs. 1–4). They detrend a 2-s-binned HEL1OS 20–50 keV lightcurve with a 251-s Savitzky–Golay filter. Morlet wavelet and FFT analysis against an AR(1) red-noise model then give a period of ~4 ± 0.6–0.7 min during roughly 01:35–01:50 UT (Fig. 5). AIA 171 Å and 1600 Å ribbon lightcurves are compared by eye with the HXR curve, and only R1's peaks line up (Fig. 6a). A 30-s STIX 20–50 keV MEM_GE image, made with stixpy, is reprojected to Earth view with SunPy and manually co-aligned to 1600 Å; it places the HXR source on R1 (Fig. 6b). An NFFF extrapolation of the HMI SHARP magnetogram at 01:24 UT shows a flux rope (twist >1), a 3D null, a sheared arcade and four QSLs (Figs. 7–9). The authors propose, without demonstrating it, that recurrent reconnection at the null and arcade drives the QPPs. For STIX/EUI flare-timing work, the paper is mainly a worked example of QPP detection plus STIX source localisation. It is also a candidate event to revisit with native STIX lightcurves and spectra.

Why these scores
Same scientific problem — 3/5

The paper detects hard X-ray QPPs and links them to a specific ribbon and to electron acceleration by reconnection, which is the QPP–acceleration–ribbon problem. However, it does no time-resolved spectroscopy, no energy-dependent timing and no statistics.

Same methodology — 3/5

It uses standard, transferable tools: Savitzky–Golay detrending, Torrence & Compo wavelet, FFT, AR(1) significance, and STIX imaging with stixpy, MEM_GE and SunPy reprojection. These are used in a basic way and with some methodological weaknesses. The NFFF extrapolation half of the paper is outside the researcher's core methods.

Same data / instruments — 3/5

STIX is used, but only for one 30-s 20–50 keV image. The QPP timing comes from Aditya-L1/HEL1OS, and EUV/UV context comes from SDO/AIA rather than EUI.

Same physical phenomenon — 4/5

The phenomena are impulsive-phase non-thermal HXR pulsations in an X-class eruptive flare, ribbon-resolved UV/EUV lightcurves, and the localisation of the pulsating source. These are the same physical phenomena the researcher targets.

Potential future utility — 3/5

The event is a ready-made case for reanalysis with STIX's own lightcurves, energy-resolved timing and spectral fitting, and possibly EUI. The paper is also a useful cautionary template for QPP-detection pitfalls. It does not provide new tools or data products, and data are available only on request.

What it offers you

  • A named X-class QPP event with timings: SOL2024-10-09, AR 13848; GOES onset/peak/end 01:25/01:56/02:43 UT; QPP interval ~01:35–01:50 UT; STIX image window 01:42:00–01:42:30 UT (Sects. 2.1–2.2, Figs. 1, 5, 6).
  • A reported period (~4 min: FFT 4.14 min, global wavelet 3.94 min, Fig. 5d) to compare against an independent STIX lightcurve analysis of the same flare.
  • A recipe for STIX source localisation in Python: stixpy with MEM_GE, background interval selection, SunPy reprojection to Earth view, and manual co-alignment to AIA 1600 Å (Sect. 2.2, Fig. 6b).
  • A ribbon-by-ribbon comparison of UV/EUV and HXR timing (AIA 171 Å chosen because the other EUV channels saturate; AIA 1600 Å for ribbons; Fig. 6a), following Clarke et al. (2021).
  • Magnetic topology context from the NFFF extrapolation: flux rope, 3D null near R1, QSL1–QSL4 mapped to the individual ribbons (Figs. 7–9). Useful if the researcher wants to link ribbon-resolved timing to topology.

Findings

Evidence The 20–50 keV HEL1OS curve (2-s bins) was detrended with a Savitzky–Golay filter of 251-s window. Wavelet and FFT both peak at ~4 min, above an AR(1) 95% level (Sect. 2.2, Fig. 5b–d).
Inference The detrending window (~4.2 min) is essentially equal to the reported period. Smoothing-based detrending is known to imprint or enhance power near the window scale, so the 4-min period may be partly filter-driven. Fig. 5b also shows only about 2–4 strong pulses, with two dominant spikes near 01:43 and 01:47 UT, so the periodicity evidence is thin.
Recommendation Do not adopt the 4-min period as established. If the event is analysed, test robustness on the non-detrended signal, for example with a Fourier power-law-plus-noise model fit in the style of Inglis & Hayes (2024), and by varying the detrending window.
high confidence
Evidence A single 30-s STIX 20–50 keV MEM_GE reconstruction (01:42:00–01:42:30 UT) was reprojected to Earth view and manually co-aligned to AIA 1600 Å brightenings. The 60% contour sits on ribbon R1 (Sect. 2.2, Fig. 6b).
Inference Manual co-alignment to bright 1600 Å features is partly circular when the conclusion is that HXR is co-spatial with a 1600 Å ribbon. A single time bin also cannot show whether the source location or footpoint balance changes from pulse to pulse.
Recommendation For this or similar events, produce STIX images per pulse and in inter-pulse intervals, and document the co-alignment procedure, for example via a thermal source or an aspect solution. Follow the approaches in Collier et al. (2024) and Lörinčík et al. (2026), which the paper cites.
medium confidence
Evidence STIX is used only for imaging. The QPP timing relies on HEL1OS 20–50 keV in one energy band, and no spectral fitting or energy-dependent timing is shown (Sect. 2.2).
Inference The event's non-thermal timing evolution is unexplored at STIX cadence and energy resolution. This includes multi-band lags, spectral-index evolution per pulse, and possible shorter-period structure on top of the 4-min modulation. Collier et al. (2023) found such fast variations common in STIX profiles.
Recommendation Check STIX data quality and coverage for SOL2024-10-09 (attenuator state, viewing geometry). If suitable, use it as a test case for energy-resolved timing and time-resolved spectroscopy of individual pulses.
medium confidence
Evidence Of the four ribbons, only R1's AIA 171 Å and 1600 Å lightcurves show peaks 'nearly coincident' with the HXR peaks (Fig. 6a, vertical dashed lines). No cross-correlation, lag values or significance are given.
Inference The ribbon attribution is qualitative. A quantitative cross-correlation or lag analysis, ideally with higher-cadence EUV such as EUI HRIEUV, would be needed to confirm where the variability originates.
Recommendation Treat Fig. 6a as a design template for ribbon-resolved lightcurve extraction. Add quantitative lag or coherence measures when applying it to EUI/STIX data.
medium confidence
Evidence The NFFF extrapolation at 01:24 UT shows a flux rope (|Tw|>1) whose leg lies under a 3D-null dome near R1, high |J|/|B| beneath the rope, and four QSLs (Figs. 7–8). The authors propose that recurrent reconnection at the null and arcade causes the QPPs (Sect. 4, Sect. 5), and state that the period cannot be derived from the extrapolation.
Inference The QPP mechanism is speculative. A static pre-flare extrapolation cannot test periodic or load/unload reconnection, and the paper itself defers this to future MHD simulations. The topology–ribbon mapping is still plausible context for why HXR concentrates at R1.
Recommendation Cite this only as a topological interpretation, not as evidence for a QPP mechanism. Use the null/QSL mapping as context if the event is reanalysed.
high confidence
Evidence Most AIA EUV channels except 171 Å saturate during this X-class flare (Sect. 2.2).
Inference This is a practical limitation for ribbon-resolved EUV timing in large flares. EUI's different exposure and cadence handling may help or may suffer similarly, depending on the observing mode.
Recommendation When selecting Major Flare Campaign events, check the EUI and AIA saturation status in advance, and plan to use 1600/1700 Å or Lyman-alpha (EUI HRILya) for ribbon timing.

Caveats

  • The Savitzky–Golay window (251 s) is nearly equal to the detected period (~240 s). Testing a smoothed-detrended series against AR(1) red noise can produce spurious significance.
  • Few cycles are present: the impulsive phase spans ~15 min, giving at most ~4 cycles of a 4-min period. The spectrum is computed over the whole flare interval.
  • STIX contributes only one 30-s image, which is manually co-aligned to the 1600 Å ribbon it is then claimed to match. There are no STIX lightcurves, spectra or energy-dependent timing.
  • Ribbon–HXR timing coincidence is judged visually (Fig. 6a), with no correlation coefficients or lags.
  • The QPP mechanism (recurrent reconnection at the null and arcade) is inferred from a single static pre-flare extrapolation and is not tested. The authors acknowledge this (Sect. 5).
  • No EUI or radio data are used. Solar Orbiter's viewing geometry for the event is not discussed.
  • Data are available only on request.

2.9 First results from PADRE/MeDDEA: X-rays from a flare and its CME

Muriel Zoë Stiefel, Säm Krucker, Olivier Limousin, Niharika Godbole et al. · submitted 2026-09-28 complementary data
accepted in Astronomy & Astrophysics, 9 pages, 6 figures, 1 table
arXiv:2609.34999PDFADS

First science paper from the PADRE/MeDDEA CubeSat spectrometer, which carries STIX flight-spare CdTe detectors: it cross-calibrates MeDDEA against ASO-S/HXI and Fermi/GBM to within ~10% (18–30 keV) for the M6 flare SOL2025-11-28T22, and uses STIX's 15°-occulted view to show that CME-associated nonthermal HXR flux is ~1% of the flare flux while its thermal energy is comparable.

The authors analyse the first large flare seen by MeDDEA (GOES M6, 28 Nov 2025). From Earth the flare was on the limb but visible; for Solar Orbiter/STIX it was occulted by 15° (occultation height 24.5 Mm). Spectral fitting with sunkit-spex (MeDDEA) and OSPEX (STIX, HXI, GBM) gives thermal-plus-thick-target fits. MeDDEA photon fluxes agree with HXI and GBM to 1.01±0.10 in 18–30 keV, after an ad hoc 5.0 s clock correction. The occulted STIX source has a nonthermal flux 0.95%±0.15% of the flare flux, the same spectral index (δ≈5.2), a cooler temperature (~13 MK vs ~29 MK) and comparable thermal energy (~4–6×10^29 vs 2.4×10^29 erg, filling factor 1). STIX CLEAN images (sub-collimators 7–10) show the source moving at ~450 km/s with the AIA 131 Å erupting flux rope. The occulted light curve is less impulsive and decays more slowly, and its thermal emission rises during the nonthermal burst in a Neupert-like way. For the researcher, the value lies in the STIX spectral/imaging workflow, the multi-instrument timing comparison, and a new STIX-cross-calibrated, high-cadence, Earth-view HXR data source with Python tooling. QPPs and fine timing are not addressed.

Same methodology — 3/5

It uses STIX spectral fitting (thermal + thick target), STIX CLEAN/MEM imaging, light-travel-time correction and inter-instrument light-curve comparison, with Python sunkit-spex fitting. These methods transfer directly, though no time-series or periodicity methods are used.

Same data / instruments — 4/5

MeDDEA gives an Earth-view STIX-like spectrometer for stereoscopic timing and spectral comparison of flares, especially occulted ones. Its high-cadence event lists could support timing or QPP work, and the occulted-flare approach can be applied across the STIX catalogue.

  • Table 1: flare versus high-corona T, EM, volume, thermal energy, density and δ, useful as a reference for energetics.

Findings

Evidence The MeDDEA 28–70 keV light curve at 0.5 s cadence has ~9% errors at peak. A 5.0 s clock shift was required to align MeDDEA with HXI and GBM nonthermal peaks (Sect. 3.1, Fig. 2).
Inference MeDDEA can supply sub-second nonthermal light curves for M-class flares. Its absolute timing is not yet reliable, so any cross-instrument timing analysis (e.g. STIX–MeDDEA lags) would need independent clock verification.
Recommendation If MeDDEA is used for timing comparisons with STIX, align it on a reference instrument (GBM/HXI) and check the status of the MeDDEA clock correction before interpreting second-level delays.
high confidence
Evidence The HXI 10–20 keV curve peaks and decays before MeDDEA's because HXI's thicker entrance window shifts its mean photon energy higher. The thermal HXR curves peak before GOES/XRS (Sect. 3.1).
Inference Apparent timing differences between instruments in nominally identical energy bands can come from instrument response rather than physics. This matters for energy-dependent timing studies that combine STIX with other instruments.
Recommendation Compare in photon space or forward-model through each instrument's response when doing multi-instrument energy-dependent timing.
high confidence
Evidence The occulted STIX 12–25 keV profile has a similar onset but is less impulsive and decays more slowly than the flare. The occulted 4–12 keV emission rises during the nonthermal burst, Neupert-like. This is interpreted as trapping, with conductive losses weaker in the high corona (Sect. 3.1, Fig. 2).
Inference Comparing occulted and unocculted temporal profiles can separate coronal-source variability from footpoint variability. That is relevant to locating where flare variability (including any QPPs) originates.
Recommendation Consider occulted STIX events with Earth-view HXR coverage as a way to isolate coronal-source temporal behaviour from ribbon emission.
medium confidence
Evidence The occulted nonthermal flux is ~1% of the flare flux, with identical δ≈5.2 in the thick-target fit. The thick- versus thin-target choice is argued from a density estimate (~2×10^9 cm^-3) and is left unresolved (Sect. 3.3, 4).
Inference This is a quantitative constraint on how the electron population is partitioned between flare and eruption. The interpretation is model-dependent, especially for thin-target or coronal sources.
Recommendation Treat the 1% fraction as thick-target-specific. When fitting coronal STIX sources, test both thick- and thin-target models.
medium confidence

Inference EUI cadence during non-campaign periods can limit joint STIX/EUI timing studies. Event selection needs to check EUI cadence, for example by favouring Major Flare Campaign periods.
Recommendation Screen candidate events for EUI high-cadence modes before building STIX–EUI timing case studies.

1.9 A New Framework for Modeling Solar Flares from MHD to Kinetic Processes

Joel C. Allred, Graham S. Kerr, Silvina E. Guidoni, Joel T. Dahlin et al. · submitted 2026-10-01 context
21 pages, 12 figures, accepted for publication by Astrophysical Journal
arXiv:2610.02149PDFADS

Chains the 2.5D ARMS MHD eruption model, the kglobal reconnection-acceleration model and RADYN+FP to inject self-consistent electron and proton distributions into a single newly reconnected flare loop, then follows transport, return currents, mirroring, chromospheric evaporation and condensation, white-light emission and HXR source height.

The authors take the flare current sheet from an ultra-high-resolution 2.5D ARMS breakout eruption (the Guidoni et al. 2016 run). They use its upstream conditions to run kglobal, which yields electron and proton spectra with κ≈2.9 (Fig. 2), most acceleration occurring within about 24 s. They inject these into a RADYN+FP loop built from an ARMS reconnected field line. Injection follows a leaky-box exponential decay with τi=20 s, chosen explicitly to match typical QPP periods (Hayes et al. 2020). FP is extended to transport multiple species coupled through the return current. Results include: - a ~24–28 MK looptop flash from return-current Ohmic heating in the first ~1 s; - evaporation upflows falling from 1.7 Mm/s to 160 km/s; - condensation of 10–30 km/s; - protons undergoing warm-target collisions in the corona and stopping sharply at the transition region; - pitch-angle-dependent electron fates under return-current, collisional and mirroring forces (Figs. 10–11); - co-spatial 30–50 keV HXR emissivity and white-light enhancement about 690 km above the photosphere, matching Krucker et al. (2015) limb observations. For STIX/EUI timing work, it is a physics-context and forward-modelling resource rather than an observational or time-series analysis. It models only a single injection episode in one symmetric loop.

Same scientific problem — 2/5

It addresses particle acceleration and the flare-loop response, the physics behind HXR/EUV timing. It does not study temporal variability, energy-dependent timing or QPPs; QPPs are used only to set the injection decay time.

Same methodology — 1/5

Purely numerical: MHD, kinetic-hybrid and radiation-hydrodynamic simulation. No time-series, spectral-fitting or imaging-analysis techniques transfer directly to STIX/EUI data analysis.

Same data / instruments — 0/5

No observational data are analysed. STIX and EUI are not used, and Solar Orbiter is mentioned only via SPICE in the introduction.

Same physical phenomenon — 4/5

Directly concerns nonthermal electron/proton spectra, transport, return-current losses, footpoint HXR source heights, evaporation and impulsive-phase heating. These are the phenomena that STIX spectra and timing diagnose.

Potential future utility — 3/5

The planned 3D, multi-loop, multi-snapshot extension and parameter studies (Sec. 4) could supply synthetic HXR/EUV lightcurves to compare with STIX/EUI timing and QPP observations. The current single-injection run yields only limited directly comparable observables.

What it offers you

  • Self-consistently derived electron and proton spectra from reconnection, κ≈2.9 for both, with acceleration timescale ~5τA≈24 s (Sec. 3.2, Fig. 2). A reference for the acceleration timescale and spectral hardness to compare with STIX-derived indices.
  • Time-dependent loop response to an exponentially decaying (τi=20 s) beam (Figs. 4–6): looptop 24–28 MK within ~1.4 s, evaporation reaching the apex at 7.3 s, apex density and temperature at 10, 20 and 40 s. Useful for sanity-checking thermal/nonthermal timing in STIX lightcurves.
  • Quantitative breakdown of return-current versus collisional versus mirroring losses for 20 keV electrons at 3° and 45° pitch angles (Figs. 9–11). Return-current losses of ~2.5 keV occur early and collisional losses of ~9 keV in the corona at t=5 s, which matters for interpreting low-energy spectral flattening and energy-dependent delays.
  • Synthetic 30–50 keV HXR emissivity height (~690 km above photosphere) co-spatial with white-light emission (Fig. 12, Sec. 3.3.2). An alternative explanation for low HXR footpoint heights invoking chromospheric compression rather than low ambient densities.
  • Temperature-dependent upflow/downflow crossover at 1–2 MK (Fig. 8), relevant to EUV ribbon/loop Doppler and intensity interpretation.

Findings

Evidence Sec. 3.3: the injection flux decays exponentially with τi=20 s, justified by QPP intervals of 'typically about 20 s' (Hayes et al. 2020). QPPs are described as potential signatures of individual reconnection bursts (Ashfield et al. 2026). Sec. 4 lists this as an assumption and notes that only one injection episode is modelled.
Inference The model treats one QPP-like burst as one injection episode, but it does not produce or test QPPs. Its value for QPP work is as a template for a single pulse's thermal and nonthermal response, not as evidence for any QPP mechanism.
Recommendation Use the single-pulse response (heating rise in <1 s, evaporation filling in ~7–10 s) as a reference when deconvolving multi-pulse STIX lightcurves. Do not cite this paper as support for a QPP mechanism.
high confidence
Evidence Sec. 3.3 and Figs. 10–11, 9: in the first ~0.5 s the return-current electric field exceeds Dreicer at the looptop. Electrons lose ~2.5 keV to return-current losses in the corona, and high-pitch-angle electrons are reversed. Later, collisional losses dominate (9 keV at t=5 s), and after ~25 s high-pitch-angle electrons are thermalized in the corona before returning.
Inference Transport effects evolve on second timescales during a single injection. Energy-dependent timing and spectral changes seen by STIX could therefore partly reflect evolving return-current and collisional conditions in the loop rather than changes at the accelerator.
Recommendation When interpreting STIX spectral flattening at low energies or energy-dependent delays early in impulsive bursts, consider return-current and coronal-density evolution as alternatives to acceleration changes. Cross-reference Alaoui et al. 2021 and Allred et al. 2020.
medium confidence
Evidence Sec. 3.3.2, Fig. 12 right: model 30–50 keV HXR emissivity peaks at the base of the compressed transition region, ~690 km above the photosphere, co-spatial with the white-light enhancement. The authors argue this matches Krucker et al. (2015) without requiring low footpoint densities.
Inference This bears on interpreting HXR footpoint heights and HXR–EUV/white-light co-spatiality. It is relevant if STIX and EUI limb or near-limb events are used to locate where emission originates in ribbons.
Recommendation If analysing near-limb STIX/EUI events, compare footpoint heights against this prediction and against Krucker et al. 2015.
medium confidence
Evidence Sec. 3.2, Fig. 2: kglobal with guide field 0.3 gives κ≈2.9 for both species. The authors note this is harder than typical solar flare spectra and that the guide field strongly controls nonthermal production (Dahlin et al. 2014; Arnold et al. 2021).
Inference The guide-field dependence offers a physical lever linking observed STIX spectral indices to reconnection geometry. The specific spectrum here is not representative of typical events.
Recommendation Treat the kglobal spectrum as illustrative only. For linking observed spectral indices to reconnection, read Arnold et al. 2021 and Yin et al. 2024a.
medium confidence
Evidence Sec. 3.3.1, Fig. 7: nonthermal protons experience warm-target collisions in the hot corona and deposit energy sharply at the transition region, pushing it lower. They carry ~10% of the injected energy flux in the leaky-box escape.
Inference Proton heating has no direct STIX signature but could alter footpoint dynamics seen by EUI. This is mostly peripheral to an electron-focused timing study.
Recommendation Note as context only; low priority.

1.3 Detection of fine-structured radio bursts during a superflare on EQ Peg

Zhanhao Zhao, Xin Cheng, Yuankun Kou, Guoyin Chen et al. · submitted 2026-09-29 weak arXiv:2609.37909PDFADS

Using FAST L-band (1.05–1.45 GHz) dynamic spectra and TESS 20-s photometry, the authors detect ~100 s of highly circularly polarized (~0.84), sub-second (~0.16 s FWHM) decimetric spike bursts near the peak of a ~10^33 erg white-light superflare on the M-dwarf binary EQ Peg (2022-09-08), interpret them as electron cyclotron maser emission, and locate candidate sources in a ZDI-based dipole field.

The paper pairs a 2.5 h FAST tracking observation with TESS Sector 56 photometry and catches a 3.9 h white-light superflare on EQ Peg. From the most intense ~100 s burst near the optical peak, 87 spikes above 5σ are identified with a local-maximum search plus multi-Gaussian fitting. Their statistics (Fig. 3, Eq. 1) are: duration ~0.16 s, frequency ~1398 MHz, polarization ~0.84, lower-limit brightness temperature ≳10^13 K. On that basis the authors argue for ECM emission. Assuming a dipole field and loss-cone or horseshoe electron distributions, they restrict the possible source heights and latitudes (Figs. 4, 10–13). No drift is seen, which gives an upper limit of ~497 MHz/s and electron energies of ≲4–7 keV if the drift is interpreted as radial motion. For the researcher this is a stellar analogue of solar decimetric spikes coinciding with flare peaks. It carries no X-ray or EUV data, does no spectral fitting, and does not analyse QPPs. Its value lies in the radio-spike detection and characterisation procedure and in the solar-stellar flare context.

Why these scores
Same scientific problem — 1/5

The topic is accelerated-electron signatures in a flare, but on a distant M dwarf with no spatial or X-ray information. It is not about solar flare temporal evolution, X-ray spectral timing or QPPs.

Same methodology — 2/5

Spike detection uses a smoothed dynamic spectrum, a 7×7 local-maximum search above 5σ, multi-Gaussian fitting of light curves and noise-threshold characterisation with non-Gaussian noise (App. B.2, Figs. 8–9). These steps are generic time-series techniques that could transfer to radio data accompanying STIX events. Nothing here is X-ray or EUV methodology.

Same data / instruments — 0/5

The data are FAST and TESS only. No STIX, EUI, RHESSI, AIA or any solar data are used.

Same physical phenomenon — 2/5

Decimetric spike bursts from flare-accelerated electrons, timed near the flare peak and produced by loss-cone or horseshoe ECM, are a known solar phenomenon tied to HXR timing. The paper explicitly discusses the solar analogy (Benz, Battaglia & Benz 2009).

Potential future utility — 1/5

Useful mainly as a citation for solar-stellar flare comparisons, or if the researcher adds radio spike data to STIX case studies. Direct reuse is unlikely.

What it offers you

  • Statistical spike parameters (Eq. 1, Fig. 3) — duration ~0.16 s, Π_c ~0.84, bandwidth ≲50 MHz — as a stellar comparison point for solar decimetric spikes.
  • A spike-identification procedure (App. B.2, Figs. 2, 8, 9): Gaussian smoothing, local-maximum detection with an explicit false-alarm fraction under non-Gaussian noise, and multi-Gaussian deconvolution of overlapping peaks.
  • A background-removal recipe for single-dish dynamic spectra (Fig. 7): a per-channel quintic fit plus subtraction of a quiet reference band, with an estimate of how much signal is lost.
  • A drift-rate detectability limit converted to an upper bound on electron energy (Sect. 3), which illustrates how resolution constrains timing diagnostics.

Findings

Evidence The most intense radio spike group (~100 s) coincides with the TESS white-light peak, and only weak bursts appear elsewhere during the 3.9 h flare (Fig. 1b, Fig. 5, App. A.3).
